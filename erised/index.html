How a story begins

There is no lobby and no character builder to get lost in. Four short questions, three openings written for you, and then the first scene with its three choices.

  1. Redeem an invitation.

    There is no public sign-up. An administrator issues single-use codes that expire after seven days; one code makes one account.

  2. Answer four questions.

    A world of your own or one of six familiar settings, a play style, a tone, and your character, ending with the line the mirror asks for: what does your heart desire?

  3. Choose one of three openings.

    The storyteller writes three openings from your answers, each with a pitch and a hook. Pick one and the first scene is written, with its three choices and a backdrop already on the page.

  4. Play by turns.

    Each turn the model proposes prose and bounded changes to the world. The server checks them, rolls any dice, and writes the journal. Paintings arrive on their own time; a turn can take a few minutes.

Play alone, or with four

A campaign is yours alone or a party of up to four. Everyone plays on their own phone or desktop; the story is one shared world.

  • Invitation codes

    The host shares a ten-character party code from the party sheet. It grants a seat in that story and nothing else; a new player still needs their own access invitation.

  • Rotating turns

    Every mode takes explicit turns, so a decision is never ambiguous, a double tap never commits twice, and a phone that loses its connection picks up where it left off.

  • Shared saved campaigns

    World state, journal and recap belong to the campaign, not the device. Pause for a week, come back, read the recap, carry on.

  • Scene alerts

    When a scene is ready, or the turn passes to you, the app says so in a quiet card. If you turn browser notifications on for a device, it can tell you while the tab is hidden.

Your kind of story

Two ways to play, and a storyteller that knows which books count.

JourneyStory first, with a d20 when it matters. Rewards, setbacks and room to recover.
Dice & destinyTake risks with a d20, four abilities and real consequences.
  • Suggested choices and a free action

    Every scene offers three choices with their stakes shown before you pick, and a line where you write whatever you like instead.

  • A d20 the server rolls

    Checks use four abilities: might, agility, wits and spirit. A natural 20 succeeds with an advantage, a natural 1 fails with a complication, and otherwise the total has to meet the difficulty. The roll is made before the storyteller writes, and a failed check always costs something.

  • Original worlds or familiar ones

    Start from a genre of your own, or from one of six literary settings: Harry Potter, Game of Thrones, Middle-earth, Star Wars, Avatar and The Witcher, with era, place and canon controls and a storyteller told which books count.

  • Health, inventory and trade-offs

    Dice & destiny adds more frequent checks, health that can reach zero without ending the story, and choices that cost something even when they are safe.

A world that remembers

Continuity is canonical world state, the recent turns, a rolling recap and recalled memories. The storyteller proposes; the server keeps the books.

  • Journal

    Every scene, every choice and every consequence, in order, with a written journal beside a visual index of the paintings.

  • Lore and recap

    Threads to follow, places, people, the rules of this world and what you have learned, on a sheet you can open from any scene.

  • Character sheet

    Name, calling, what your heart desires, health, four abilities and what you carry. A character can be saved and brought into the next story.

  • Pause, resume, export

    Pause a story and return later; download the whole journal as Markdown. A story that ends keeps its journal, and a saved scene can be read offline.

Painted while you play

Scenes are illustrated by a local image backend after the prose is written, so reading never waits on a picture. Portraits are never generated.

  • Backdrop first, painting later

    A shared scenic backdrop appears at once; the scene’s own painting fades in over it when it is ready. That can take several minutes, and the page says so rather than pretending.

  • Eight moods

    A helper model names one of eight fixed atmospheres for each scene. They are fixed themes, never model-written styles, and the whole effect has an off switch.

  • Never a portrait

    Paintings show unpeopled places: terrain, architecture, weather and light. Faces are never generated; you and your party stay as arched monograms.

  • Sharpened on the server

    Paintings are made at 512 by 768 and super-resolved to 2560 pixels on the CPU with a bundled network. You see the original first and the sharp copy when it arrives.

8-bit interludes

Two distinct solo modes sit beside Journey and Dice & destiny: a Platformer and an Explorer, built on Phaser, with stages the storyteller plans and a compiler checks.

  • Platformer

    Left and right, or A and D; Space to jump; E to talk or pull a lever. Land on enemies to defeat them, finish the stage’s objectives and reach the gate. Trail camps heal and become respawn points.

  • Explorer

    Arrows or WASD; E to talk, open chests and throw switches. Turn-based encounters with Attack, Guard, Heal and Flee; keys, shards and gates to open.

  • Worlds that grow

    One saved stage at a time. Completing the objectives opens the exit and grants XP, coins and a keepsake once. The storyteller plans the next stage; the compiler owns physics, combat and rewards, and rejects anything unreachable.

  • Original pixels

    Textures are drawn in code; no copyrighted game art is loaded. Sound starts muted. Touch controls, pause, an objective checklist and a stage map on every stage.

The private mirror

When a story ends, the mirror offers a reflection: what you chose, what chance changed, what you did next. It is private to you, every finding links to the scenes behind it, and it is not a clinical assessment.

  • Six categories

    Uncertainty, agency, trust, care, values and flexibility. From the third scene on, roughly every fourth scene can carry a small test of one of them, chosen on a shuffled cycle rather than from a guess about you.

  • Evidence, not scores

    At most four findings, each citing the decisions it rests on and labelled tentative, mixed or repeated. Coverage is shown as counts of observations. No trait percentiles, no diagnoses, no personality badges.

  • You can disagree

    Each finding ends with a question, a small experiment to try, and a place to say whether the reading fits, partly fits, does not fit, or was roleplay. Your corrections inform the next update.

  • Yours to delete

    The reflection never appears in campaign exports, in prompts to the storyteller or in admin metrics. Deleting it removes the observations and the report; the story stays in your journal.

Invite-only, on purpose

Erised runs on Team AER’s own machines against local models; there is no external inference dependency, no billing and no public sign-up. The repository is private. If you would like to play, ask, and an administrator sends a single-use access invitation that expires in seven days.

  • Accounts are a local username and password; no third-party identity provider.
  • Browsers never hold model credentials; every model call goes from the server to the gateway.
  • Works on phones and desktops; a saved scene can be read offline, playing needs the server.
