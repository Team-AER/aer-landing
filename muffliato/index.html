Keep your words off the transcript.

Muffliato adds a quiet layer to recorded speech. People hear the same words. Speech-recognition software returns an empty transcript.

Built for rooms where the recording isn’t yours to stop

Blocking the bot and asking for consent cover most meetings. Muffliato is for the recording you cannot prevent: a counterparty who records anyway, a review copy that leaves the building, a voice that is already out there.

  • First

    Confidential meetings in regulated organisations

    Law firms, clinics, boards and universities. Unconsented AI transcription is now live litigation risk, and the notetaker bans already in place stop the invited bot, not the other side’s recorder.

    Who signs: security, general counsel, compliance. Fits beside Zoom and Teams bot blocking and SaaS governance.

  • Second

    Voice talent, narrators and audiobook publishers

    The one adjacent market with published willingness to pay for voice protection. Delivered audio can carry the protection, so the words are not lifted into training sets or quote farms.

    Who signs: agencies and publishers, with unions as endorsers. Fits beside likeness and takedown subscriptions.

  • Third

    Pre-release review copies

    Screeners and dailies already carry per-copy forensic watermarks. A review copy tolerates an audible-in-principle mark, and the studio controls the encode. No industry standard yet covers AI extraction from these files.

    Who signs: screener and dailies platforms, studio post-production security.

  • Fourth

    Confidential interviews and source material

    Documentary and investigative producers share raw interviews with freelancers and commissioners. Source protection is a legal exposure with a budget behind it.

    Who signs: legal and standards teams at news organisations and commissioning broadcasters.

This order comes from Team AER’s own demand research: observed budget first, observed pain second. Podcast networks, archives and broadcasters were studied and left out; none objected to machine transcription, and several license their speech to AI buyers instead.

Why this, why now

Thirty months of signals show buyers acting against machine transcription itself. Every answer they have today either blocks the bot or asks for consent. None acts on the audio.

  1. Aug 2025

    Four lawsuits against Otter.ai over recording and training on meeting audio. Chapman University blocks Read AI in Zoom and Teams; Harvard restricts assistants to contracted tools.

  2. Dec 2025

    The New York City Bar requires client consent before AI recording. A patient sues Sharp HealthCare over an ambient scribe.

  3. Mar 2026

    VCU moves Zoom to authorised SDKs only, with zero third-party notetakers approved. Granola raises $125M for a notetaker: the counter-market keeps growing.

  4. May 2026

    Nine biometric-privacy class actions by Chicago journalists, podcasters and narrators against Adobe, Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ElevenLabs, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Samsung.

  5. Aug 2026

    Wiretap, CIPA and BIPA claims against Otter.ai survive dismissal. Zoom ships an admin dashboard to block meeting bots.

  6. Oct 2026

    Exa indexes 3.2 million podcast episodes with full transcripts. No publisher objects, which is why podcasts are not on the list above.

What happens to a recording

Muffliato works on the audio itself, before it leaves your hands. It does not depend on who plays the file later or what they run it through.

  1. 1

    You hand it a recording

    A finished file: an interview, a screener’s audio track, a narrated chapter. Nothing about the speakers or the words is needed.

  2. 2

    It adds a quiet layer shaped for the recogniser

    A model listens to the whole clip and writes a second, very quiet signal over it, designed to confuse speech-recognition software rather than people. The layer is held within a strict loudness limit.

  3. 3

    You get the same recording back

    Same length, same format, same words to a listener. Transcription software is given the file and returns nothing.

The empty quadrant

Everything on the market today either acts after a copy exists or only governs web pages. Nothing governs audio delivered as a file, a stream or a screener.
